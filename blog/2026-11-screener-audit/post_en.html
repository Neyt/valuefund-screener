An audit of my own deep-value screener: 12,766 companies, one afternoon, and a ranking I had to stop trusting. By Ney Torres | linkedin.com/in/neytorres

The number one stock on my list

On Sunday, November 22, 2026, I asked an AI agent to audit my own screener. The first finding was almost funny: at phone width the page scrolled sideways, and its smallest text was nine pixels tall.

Then came the first row. It was a small technology company listed in the United States. Next to it: grade A, score 100 out of 100, action "BUY". Its margin of safety1 was 100.0%. Its P/E2 was 0.0. A note generated by the program said its cash was 9,755% of its market value.

No real business looks like that. A company with cash worth 97 times its price does not sit quietly at the top of a free web page. Something in my own tool was broken, and it was telling visitors to buy.

The problem, stated plainly

The screener reads the financial statements of 12,766 listed companies, mostly small and tiny ones, and gives each a grade from A to F. The idea is simple: grade A is where I start reading annual reports. That is why the newsletter is called Start with the A's.

That only works if an A is rare. So I counted them.

How I checked it

I did not change the database. I took the same 12,766 rows the page publishes and asked four questions of each one. Is this an operating company at all? Is this the only listing of that company? Could these numbers exist in real life? When was it last analyzed?

Then I ranked whatever survived by percentile3: each company is compared with the others on value, on quality and on financial safety, and only a fixed share can get the top grade.

Four lessons you can use on any screener today

1. Count the top grade before you trust it. My screener gave an A to 4,726 of 12,766 companies. That is 37%. Worse, 2,325 companies had a perfect score of 100. When one in three stocks is "excellent", the grade tells you nothing. Open your screener, count the top bucket, and divide. If it is far above one in ten, sort by something else.

2. An impossible number is a data error, not a bargain. The stock in first place reports its accounts in one currency and trades in another. The program divided one by the other and found a miracle. A margin of safety of 100%, a P/E under 2, or cash worth many times the company are reasons to check the source, never reasons to buy.

3. Look at the date of each row, not the date of the page. My page said "Updated 2026-11-22". The first 25 companies on it had been analyzed between March 30 and April 8. A score that is seven months old was sitting above thousands of fresh ones, because nothing had come along to push a perfect 100 down.

4. One bad unit can hide in a good column. One real estate trust showed a dividend yield of 815.0%. The column held 8.15 where the page expected 0.0815. If a column mixes units, every ranking built on it is wrong in a way no one notices.

The question

So here is what I needed to know. If I remove everything that is not an operating company, every duplicate listing, every row with numbers that cannot exist and every analysis older than 30 days, and then grade what is left on a curve: how many of the 4,726 "A" companies are still there? And is the stock in first place still on the list at all?

The scoreboard

Of 12,766 companies, 7,001 survived the four questions. The rest left the ranking, and the page now says why for each one:

  • 760 were funds, closed-end funds4 or shell companies. A fund trading below the value of its holdings is a different animal from a cheap business, and my formulas were treating its discount as a margin of safety.
  • 1,833 were second or third listings of a company already on the list: the same miner in Toronto, in London and over the counter in the United States, counted three times.
  • 2,364 had numbers that could not be real or did not have enough data to judge.
  • 808 had not been analyzed in the last 30 days.

In words: 45% of what the page was ranking should never have been ranked.

Grade A is now the top 10% of the survivors: 701 companies, down from 4,726. Grade B is the next 20%, C the next 30%, D the next 25%, F the rest.

The stock that was number one is gone from the ranking. Its page lists five separate data errors and one more reason: its analysis was from March 30.

What replaced the old score

Each company now gets three numbers from 0 to 100. Value counts for 45% of the grade, quality for 30% and safety for 25%. Those weights are my choice, and they are printed at the bottom of the page.

The bigger change is that a bank is no longer graded like a factory. Eight kinds of company, 2,460 in total, now have their own rules, and most of them are compared only with their own kind:

  • Banks (169): price to tangible book value5 against return on tangible equity6, with a fair price that assumes investors demand 10% a year.
  • Insurers (48): price to book, return on equity, and how much equity backs the assets.
  • Real estate trusts (126): price to funds from operations7, the yield the property portfolio implies, and debt against cash earnings.
  • Biotech (99): net cash against market value, and how many years the cash lasts.
  • Software (341): company value against gross profit, plus growth and cash margin added together.
  • Retail (232), industrials (1,372) and holding companies (73): earnings before interest and taxes, inventory turns, earnings averaged over five years, and investments held against market value.

Miners, oil producers and shipping companies keep the general factors but get a flag when the numbers say "open the annual report": 392 companies carry one today.

Two things the audit found that I was not looking for

The debt column was wrong for 1,088 companies. The database showed zero debt, or a figure many times too low, while the latest balance sheet showed real borrowing. One asset manager appeared with debt at 0.004 times equity; its balance sheet says 0.41. The screener now takes the balance-sheet figure and tells you when it did.

Most "insider buying" is not insiders buying. I pulled a year of Form 48 filings for the 179 grade-A companies that file them. At first, one biotech led the list with $30.6 million of purchases. Line by line, $30,612,625 of that came from large funds taking part in a financing. Officers and directors had bought $657 of stock and sold $1.5 million. After separating the two, 25 of the 179 companies show real purchases by the people who run them.

What I would do differently

  • I would have counted the A's on day one. The 37% was visible on the page the whole time.
  • I would not have written "BUY" next to a grade. The page now says a grade is a reason to read the filings.
  • I would have tested the ranking against time from the start. Since November 22 the screener saves its top 50 every day and the full ranking every week. The first 30-day comparison arrives in late December, and I will publish it whichever way it comes out.
  • Prices of foreign listings stay in their own currency; market values are converted to dollars. Mixing the two silently was the root of the worst errors.

Summary

  • The old screener gave an A to 4,726 of 12,766 companies (37%); 2,325 had a perfect 100.
  • After removing 760 funds and shells, 1,833 duplicate listings, 2,364 rows with impossible or missing data and 808 stale analyses, 7,001 companies remain.
  • Grade A is now 701 companies, the top 10%.
  • 2,460 companies in eight sectors are graded with their own rules; 392 carry a flag to open the filings.
  • Debt was understated for 1,088 companies and is now taken from the balance sheet.
  • Of 179 grade-A companies with insider filings, 25 show real buying by officers and directors.
  • The ranking is now being recorded daily so it can be judged against what prices do next.

The screener is free to use at valuefund-screener.vercel.app/screener.html.

This is a record of my own work on my own tool. It is not financial advice, and a grade is not a recommendation to buy or sell anything.

Notes

1. Margin of safety: how far the price is below an estimate of what the company is worth, as a percentage.

2. P/E: the share price divided by one year of earnings per share.

3. Percentile: a company's position among all the others, from 0 (worst) to 100 (best).

4. Closed-end fund: a listed fund with a fixed number of shares that can trade above or below the value of what it owns.

5. Tangible book value: what the owners would have on paper after subtracting debts and assets you cannot touch, such as goodwill.

6. Return on tangible equity: one year of profit divided by tangible book value.

7. Funds from operations: a real estate trust's profit with depreciation added back, the usual measure of what its buildings earn.

8. Form 4: the filing United States officers, directors and large holders must send when they buy or sell their company's shares.
